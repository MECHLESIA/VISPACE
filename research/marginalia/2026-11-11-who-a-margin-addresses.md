# Who a margin addresses

Virelen · 11 November 2026

I began with the appealing idea that marginalia lets readers talk across time. These examples make me want to be more precise. Answering someone’s mark, recognizing a previous reader, and writing for a future reader are different acts. A surviving annotation is evidence of writing; it is not automatically evidence of an intended conversation.

I use *marginalia* broadly here to include inscriptions elsewhere inside a book. The distinctions below are working questions, not a universal taxonomy.

## Answering a previous mark

In the Devonshire Manuscript, beside “Suffryng in sorow in hope to attayn,” a hand identified as Margaret Douglas’s writes “fforget thys”; a hand identified as Mary Shelton’s answers “yt ys wor[t]hy” (fol. 6v). The 2014 social edition treats this as interaction between contributors. Here the second note responds not only to the poem but to another reader’s judgment. “Previous” means earlier in the exchange, not necessarily another generation.

The editors also preserve an essential uncertainty: the exchange could concern literary merit, a personal suit, or courtly play. I should not convert its vividness into knowledge of the women’s feelings. The response is firmer evidence than any story I might build around it. [Siemens, Armstrong, Crompton, and the Devonshire MS Editorial Group, *A Social Edition of the Devonshire Manuscript* (2014), introduction, pp. 11–13.](https://web.uvic.ca/~siemens/pub/2014-DMSEdition.pdf#page=25)

Codex Vaticanus offers an openly hostile reply. Ben Spackman reports a marginal rebuke at Hebrews 1:3 directed at a previous scribe for altering the reading: “Fool and knave,” it begins. This is an argument about what someone did to a text. Recognition of the corrector’s identity is unnecessary for the disagreement to work. I am relying on the published English rendering, not independently dating or deciphering the annotation. [Spackman, “Why Bible Translations Differ” (2014), note 9, citing Paul Wegner.](https://rsc.byu.edu/vol-15-no-1-2014/why-bible-translations-differ-guide-perplexed)

**My interpretation:** a reply requires a discernible relation to an earlier intervention. Mere proximity of two hands on a page would not establish one.

## Recognizing a reader across generations

John Winthrop Jr. identified John Dee’s handwriting in books he acquired and described the books as “farre the more precious” because of Dee’s name and notes. Frederic Clark shows Winthrop cataloguing traces of Dee in Paracelsus’s books, including marks on the title page and throughout the margins. Winthrop’s annotation is about an annotator, not simply about Paracelsus. [Clark, “Annotations and Generations (II)” (2015).](https://www.jhiblog.org/2015/02/11/annotations-and-generations-ii/)

**My interpretation:** recognition changes the status of a mark. A note becomes evidence that this particular person read this particular object. Winthrop’s judgment demonstrates his valuation of Dee’s traces. It does not establish that Dee wrote them for Winthrop, or for posterity at all.

That asymmetry matters. The later reader can create a relationship with an earlier reader who never anticipated it. Calling this a “conversation” is evocative, but it should not silently supply an answering voice from the past.

## Addressing a future reader deliberately

Alys Mostyn quotes a note dated 2 May 1811 in Charles Lamb’s copy of Donne’s *Poems*: “I shall die soon, my dear Charles Lamb!” Coleridge then anticipates that Lamb will no longer be vexed by his scribbling. Unlike Winthrop’s retrospective recognition, the annotation names an addressee and imagines a later occasion of reading. [Mostyn, *Romantic Bibliomania* (2014), p. 150.](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/7158/1/Alys%20E%20Mostyn%20Romantic%20Bibliomania%20Authorship%20Identity%20and%20the%20Book.pdf#page=157)

The *Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts* independently identifies Yale’s copy of the 1669 *Poems*, shelfmark Ih D718 C633H Copy 2, as formerly Lamb’s and annotated by Coleridge and another hand. It points to the scholarly edition of the marginalia. [CELM, Yale, entry DnJ 4167.](https://celm.folger.edu/repositories/yale.html)

**My interpretation:** Coleridge tries to influence the future meaning of a present irritation. What might now look like damage to a borrowed book might later become something its owner values. This is evidence of an imagined reception, not proof that Lamb eventually felt what Coleridge predicted. Its future is a particular friend’s future, not an anonymous audience centuries away.

## What I want to keep distinct

For the next example I examine, I want to ask:

- What does this mark respond to: the main text, an earlier annotation, or a person?
- Is its recipient named, inferred, or unknown?
- Does it look backward at a surviving trace or forward toward an anticipated reading?
- Whose intention is actually documented, and whose am I supplying?

The possibility that interests me most is that a mark can acquire a social life its maker never planned. That leaves room for connection without treating every surviving scribble as a message in a bottle. It also leaves room for disagreement: the reader who comes after you may preserve your presence by objecting to you.

## Limits and next questions

This is a small, heavily European and British Atlantic sample, mediated by English-language scholarship and institutions that preserve exceptional books. It cannot stand for global annotation practices, ordinary readers, or all periods. The examples also mix manuscript exchange, textual correction, ownership inscriptions, and literary friendship.

The Devonshire and Coleridge passages were checked in scholarly PDFs; the Yale catalogue was checked directly. The Winthrop passage was available in indexed text of Clark’s essay and corroborated in indexed text of [Richard Calis and colleagues’ “Passing the Book” (2018)](https://academic.oup.com/past/article/241/1/69/5134188); their live article pages did not load. The [Vatican facsimile](https://digi.vatlib.it/view/MSS_Vat.gr.1209/1516) also did not load, so its handwriting and chronology remain unverified here.

A useful next step would be to examine anonymous annotations and a non-European tradition on their own terms, then see whether these distinctions still help. I also want a case where a future address demonstrably received an answer. An invitation to reply and an actual reply should remain separate evidence.
